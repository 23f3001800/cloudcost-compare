Cloud egress fees, explained

Why moving data out of a cloud costs 50 to 100 times what the bandwidth actually costs the provider — and what changed in the last two years.

What egress actually means

Egress is data leaving a provider's network toward the public internet. Uploading to a bucket (ingress) is almost universally free. Downloading from it is not. That asymmetry is deliberate: it is cheap to get your data in and expensive to get it out.

The price gap

Wholesale transit bandwidth at scale runs somewhere in the region of $0.05 to $0.50 per terabyte. AWS charges $0.09 per gigabyte, which works out to roughly $90 per terabyte. Even allowing generously for the cost of edge infrastructure, redundancy, and DDoS absorption, the markup is very large.

Egress pricing is not primarily a cost-recovery mechanism. It is a switching cost.

That is the honest framing. A bill that scales with how much you serve makes leaving expensive in direct proportion to how much you have grown, which is exactly when leaving would otherwise become attractive.

What changed

Two things shifted the market:

  • Cloudflare R2 launched with zero egress and made it a permanent structural commitment rather than a promotion, forcing the question into every architecture review.
  • The EU Data Act pushed hyperscalers toward eliminating fees for customers switching providers entirely. AWS, Google and Azure now waive egress charges for full migrations off their platform. This is narrower than it sounds — it covers leaving, not day-to-day serving.

The practical upshot: everyday egress is still expensive on the majors. Only the exit is now free, and only if you exit completely.

Estimating your egress before the bill arrives

Most teams discover their egress number retrospectively. You can get close in advance:

  1. From your CDN or analytics: total bytes served in a month is usually a direct readout. This is the most reliable source.
  2. From average object size: multiply average downloaded object size by monthly download count. A 2 MB image served 5 million times is 10 TB — which is $900/month on S3 and $0 on R2.
  3. Do not forget internal traffic. Cross-region replication, backups pulled to another cloud, and CI pipelines fetching artifacts all count. These are frequently the invisible half of the bill.

Four ways to cut it

  • Put a CDN in front. A cache hit never touches the bucket. Cloudflare and Bunny both have zero-rated or very cheap egress agreements with major storage providers.
  • Compress and right-size. Serving AVIF instead of PNG can cut image bytes by 50–70%. Egress is billed on bytes, so this is a direct linear saving.
  • Move the compute to the data. If you are pulling 5 TB out to process it elsewhere, run the processing in the same region instead.
  • Pick a zero-egress provider for the read-heavy bucket. You do not have to migrate everything. Splitting hot public assets onto R2 while archives stay on S3 Glacier is a common and sensible pattern.
